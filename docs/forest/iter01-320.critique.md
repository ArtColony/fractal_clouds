1. **Give the trees wider, continuous crowns.** The crop shows extremely thin, nearly parallel columns with foliage broken into little knots along a pole. Many read roughly 10–20 times taller than their crown width. Aim for a varied population mostly around **4–7:1**, retaining some slender crowded trees. A photographed spruce has a pointed leader, an irregular taper, and layered branches that generally broaden downward. Let branch tips droop and overlap; avoid both cylindrical crowns and perfect triangular outlines.

2. **Replace the uniform picket texture with trees competing for space.** Too many trees have similar heights, widths, and vertical emphasis. Introduce dominant trees, shorter suppressed trees, and occasional young groups. Vary neighbouring heights by roughly **15–30%**, with larger differences around gaps. In closed stands, crowns should touch or overlap; openings should form irregular connected patches. Wider, fuller trees belong along sunny edges, while crowded interior trees should have narrower crowns and longer exposed lower trunks.

3. **Light whole crowns before adding needle detail.** The crop’s scattered yellow-green flecks against almost black columns make the foliage look granular and brittle. A photograph would show coherent illuminated branch surfaces, dark undersides, and readable transitions around each crown. Use restrained **olive or grey-green highlights**, deeper green interiors, and cooler blue-green shade. Reduce isolated bright speckles and reserve the darkest values for small, genuinely occluded spaces.

4. **Make the ground belong to the forest.** The exposed floor reads as broad, smooth grey-brown strips between upright trees, with weak contact between roots, shade, and terrain. Beneath dense spruce, expect a substantially darker floor: muted brown litter, subdued moss, fallen branches, and overlapping shadows. Keep brighter grass mainly in openings and along edges. Ground visibility should increase gradually where the canopy thins, rather than appearing as clean lanes through it.

5. **Break up the forest boundary and simplify distant trees.** Across the valley, the forest becomes a fairly uniform dark blanket with a conspicuous banded edge. A real forest would follow gullies, sheltered slopes, and rocky interruptions, with fingers of trees and isolated groups extending into meadow. Near the upper limit, trees should become shorter and more scattered. At distance, merge individuals into softly varied canopy masses, progressively **lighter, cooler, and lower in contrast**; preserve individual spikes mainly along silhouettes.

The single worst non-tree problem is the massif’s **melted, folded rock surface**: its broad rounded grooves need more angular fractures, ledges, and broken scree transitions to resemble photographed alpine rock.

TREE REALISM: 4/10.
