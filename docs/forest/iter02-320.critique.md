1. **Broaden the crowns and soften their outlines.** Many trees read as narrow, ragged columns, roughly **8–12 times taller than wide**, with long exposed tips and stacked horizontal fragments. Aim for most mature spruce crowns around **4–6:1**, retaining a few slender specimens. A photograph would show fuller lower and middle crowns, irregular drooping branch skirts, occasional gaps, and shorter, finer leaders.

2. **Build a connected canopy with varied heights.** Too many trees remain individually legible from tip to base, producing a field of upright spikes. Give neighboring trees overlapping crowns, with typical trunk spacing around **half to one crown diameter** in dense stands. Vary neighboring heights by roughly **20–30%**, with occasional emergent trees and groups of shorter regeneration. Real forest has an uneven canopy surface with substantial shared masses of foliage.

3. **Replace the evenly scattered placement with ecological patches.** The meadow margins look sprinkled with similarly sized trees, while the foreground contains conspicuous flat charcoal gaps. Concentrate trees into irregular groves, leave larger coherent openings, and put younger trees along some edges. Visible ground should carry subdued brown litter, roots, rocks, and patchy moss or grass; beneath closed canopy it should mostly disappear into broken, deep shadow.

4. **Give crowns coherent light and richer green shadows.** The close trees have pale olive flecks over gray-green bodies, but those flecks often resemble surface noise rather than illuminated branches. Use darker, restrained **blue-green interiors**, warmer olive-green sun-facing branch surfaces, and dark contact shadows between overlapping trees. A photograph would show broad lighting differences across each crown, with fine needle highlights subordinate to that structure.

5. **Make distant forest read as masses, and make its boundaries respond to terrain.** The mid-distance crop becomes a curtain of similarly thin, pale vertical strokes. Merge those trees into overlapping groups with darker pockets and softer, less distinct individual crowns. Atmospheric haze should reduce contrast without turning every tree equally gray. Break the forest edge into tongues, gaps, and small outlying groups following gullies, ridges, and meadow conditions; exposed upper edges should include shorter, more irregular trees.

The single worst non-tree problem is the **melted, softly folded appearance of the mountain rock**: it needs sharper structural breaks and more convincing fractured faces.

TREE REALISM: 4/10.
